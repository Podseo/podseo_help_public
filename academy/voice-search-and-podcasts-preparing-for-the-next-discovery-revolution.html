Voice Requests and Podcasts

On this page

Voice Requests and Podcasts: Use Clear, Conversational Wording ​

A spoken request can reveal how a listener describes a topic: “Find a podcast about starting a business while working full-time.” Use that language as a prompt for clearer metadata. It does not require a separate voice-SEO formula.

This guide does not claim that voice search is growing at a particular rate or that podcast platforms report a distinct voice-search audience. A conversational query, mobile visit or commute-time play is not proof of voice discovery.

Turn a spoken question into a useful topic ​

  1. Collect actual questions from listeners, interviews or community discussions.
  2. Identify the subject and the listener's situation.
  3. Check that the episode answers the question.
  4. Write a title and opening description that make that scope clear.

For example:

Listener's questionEpisode topicPossible title
“How do I start a business while keeping my job?”Planning a side business around employmentStarting a Side Business While Working Full-Time
“What can I listen to while learning Spanish?”Practice material for beginner learnersBeginner Spanish: A Short Conversation About Travel

These are invented examples of wording, not measured search demand. A natural-sounding phrase may have few searches; relevance still comes first.

Turn a question into clear wording

An example, not measured search demand.

  1. Listener’s question“How do I start a business while keeping my job?”
  2. Topic + situationA side business alongside employment.
  3. Check the episodeDoes the episode actually answer the listener’s question?
  4. Make the scope clear“Starting a Side Business While Working Full-Time”

A natural phrase does not prove voice discovery.

Try this in ten minutes: pick one real listener question and an episode that answers it. Write a plain-language title and a one-sentence description, then ask someone who has not heard the episode what they expect to learn. If their answer matches your content, record the original wording and any available search position before making a change. You can start with one episode; there is no need to rewrite your entire catalogue.

Make names and descriptions easy to understand ​

Read the show name, episode title and opening description aloud. Ask someone unfamiliar with the show what they expect to hear. Revise ambiguous wording, unexplained abbreviations or spellings that make the name difficult to repeat.

Use accurate host, guest and topic names. Keep the core description consistent across your host, website and platform profiles, and correct outdated information when your show changes.

Know what each assistant is being asked to do ​

“Play my latest episode of [show]” is a request to find a known show. “Recommend a podcast for a beginner gardener” asks for a recommendation. A spoken web question can return a website rather than a podcast-app result. These are different tasks and need not use the same sources or ranking system.

Check the actual assistant and device your audience uses. A result from one device or account is a spot check, not evidence that all listeners receive the same answer. AI Mentions records assistant recommendation observations; it is not a voice-search ranking report.

Use transcripts and show notes for readers too ​

Transcripts, descriptive chapters and clear show notes help people navigate an episode and reuse its information. Check names and specialist terms in automatic transcripts. Whether that text is searched depends on the platform; do not assume every voice assistant indexes the spoken audio or rewards a particular episode structure.

For web pages, answer the relevant question clearly and provide the episode's context and useful links. Do not promise a featured snippet, a spoken answer or a podcast recommendation as the result.

Measure only what the source reports ​

Use Keyword Tracker to review observed text-search positions for tracked terms. Use Analytics for source-reported plays, views and discovery breakdowns, keeping their units and dates separate.

Neither a long question in a search report nor a rise in mobile visits identifies a voice-search session. Without explicit source attribution, voice discovery remains unavailable rather than an estimate to reconstruct from timing or query style.

For a practical experiment, record the old and new wording, the date of the change, and the positions or listening reports you can actually observe. Note other releases and promotions. Ask listeners how they found you if that context matters, treating their answers as self-reports rather than complete attribution.

Put this into practice ​
